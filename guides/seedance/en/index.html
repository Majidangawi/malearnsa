01

What Seedance 2.0 can actually do

Before you write a single word, know your instrument. Seedance 2.0 generates video three ways — and it's the third one that separates amateurs from directors.

ModeYou give itBest for
Text-to-Video (T2V)A written prompt onlyInventing a scene from scratch
Image-to-Video (I2V)A still image + promptAnimating an exact frame you already love
Reference-to-Video (R2V)Up to 9 images and/or 3 videos + promptConsistency — same face, outfit, product, motion, or style across shots

What it's genuinely strong at — lean into these, because the model rewards it:

  • Complex instruction following — action, expression, camera, and lighting honored together in one prompt.
  • Material realism — natural reflections, grain, fabric, skin, and light. Cinematic CG texture.
  • Professional shot language — close-ups, orbit shots, bullet time, stable multi-shot sequences.
  • Subject consistency — identity holds across a clip, with fewer structural errors and visual jumps.
  • Emotional nuance — real, readable facial expression and emotional flow, not mask-like.
  • Autonomous dialogue & storytelling — give an outline, and it can arrange characters, lines, and beats.
  • Voice, music & SFX — distinct character voices, fitting score, sound matched to action.
Know your limits

Every clip is 4–15 seconds. Keep a single prompt to 3–5 shots — push past that and each shot loses detail. These two numbers shape every decision below.

02

The director's mindset

Most people write prompts like a tourist describing a postcard: "a beautiful cinematic shot of a woman, moody, atmospheric, 4K." The model gives back exactly what that is — generic.

A director doesn't describe a picture. A director specifies a shot. The single most valuable habit you can build:

The one rule under all the others

Specific beats vague, every time. "Slow dolly-in from a waist shot to a close-up" will always beat "the camera moves closer." The word "cinematic" on its own is lazy — name the shot, the light, the lens, the motion.

Hold this sentence in your head as you write:

The whole craft in one line
Cinematic prompting = directing + editing + cinematography + storytelling, combined.

Every block you're about to learn is one of those four hats. Miss a hat, and the shot feels flat.

03

The core cinematic structure

A strong prompt carries eight blocks, in this order. The model is most sensitive to what comes first, so front-load whatever matters most to your shot.

The structure
[Scene] + [Subject] + [Action] + [Shot Type] + [Lighting] + [Camera Movement] + [Style / Quality] + [Audio]

Notice the two blocks most people fuse — and shouldn't:

  • Shot Type is composition — what's in the frame (wide, medium, close-up, over-the-shoulder).
  • Camera Movement is motion — how the camera behaves (dolly, tracking, handheld, orbit).

A medium over-the-shoulder can be locked-off or a slow push-in. Decide both, separately. That's the difference between a snapshot and a shot.

04

Six rules that never change

  • Build simple → complex. Start lean, add detail, watch what the model does, iterate.
  • Keep the order. Scene → Subject → Action → Shot → Atmosphere. Structure helps the model parse intent.
  • Front-load the keyword. The most important element goes at the start of the prompt.
  • Specific over vague. Name the move, the lens, the light — never settle for "cinematic."
  • No contradictions. Don't ask for "perfectly still" and "rapid motion" in the same breath.
  • One language per prompt. Don't mix languages mid-prompt; it confuses the parse.
05

The eight blocks, in depth

Each block has a mental model and the dimensions worth naming. This is where flat prompts become directed ones.

▸Scene comes first

It grounds everything that follows. Define location, scale, time of day, weather, and background elements. "A street" is nothing. "A narrow rain-slick alley at blue hour, neon signs bleeding onto wet asphalt, steam rising from a grate" is a world.

▸Subject must be concrete

This is your insurance against drift. Define physical traits, clothing, condition, and emotional state. "Condition" is the one people forget — wet, dusty, exhausted, freshly-pressed. It tells the model the story already happening on the body.

▸Action creates time

Movement is what gives a clip its duration. Define movement, sequence, interaction, and emotional behavior. For a chain of actions, use chronological connectors — first… then… finally — so the beats link cleanly instead of blurring.

▸Shot type controls composition

Name the framing and the viewer's relationship to the subject: extreme wide, wide, medium, close-up, over-the-shoulder. An over-the-shoulder, for instance, instantly defines perspective and tension between two subjects.

▸Lighting defines emotion

Light carries mood more than any adjective. Reach for cinematographer's terms — volumetric lighting, rim light, backlight, neon reflections, high contrast. The same face reads tender in soft backlight and dangerous in hard side-light.

▸Camera movement creates the cinematic feel

Always specify how the camera behaves: dolly, tracking, pan, tilt, handheld, aerial, POV, or slow motion. A static frame reads like stock footage; a slow push-in reads like cinema.

▸Style & quality — the finish

Define realism level, resolution, lens character, and texture. Seedance is stable across a huge range — photorealistic film, Van Gogh impasto, claymation, 1990s VHS, ink wash. Name the register precisely and it will hold it.

▸Audio completes the scene

Define ambient sound, sound effects, music, and dialogue. Write dialogue directly in quotes — the model matches the voice to the character's described age, gender, and personality. Match SFX to on-screen action: eating earns chewing sounds, a clash earns metal.

▸Emotional expression — the soul

Seedance reads emotion close to a real performer. Direct it: facial expression, emotional shifts, body language. "Her composure cracks — the smile holds a second too long, then her eyes give her away" is a performance, not a face.

▸Physical realism — name the physics

Stating how things obey physics raises realism more than any "8K" tag. Include gravity, motion, impact, environmental interaction — "the bottle rolls, bounces, hits a step and shatters, balls scattering across the ground" gives the model rules to honor.

06

Multi-shot & the timed storyboard

To direct more than one shot in a single clip, stop writing prose and start writing a storyboard. Mark each shot with a bracketed time range. One bracket = one shot.

The timed-block method
[0–2s] [shot type] — [who] — [action] [2–4s] ... [4–6s] ... [6–9s] ... [9–12s] ... [12–15s] [climax]
  • Each block is one shot. Describe who is where, doing what, filmed how.
  • Stay within 3–5 shots even with fine timestamps — group beats, don't fragment.
  • Uneven intervals are intentional. Short blocks early (fast cuts), longer holds late, as tension resolves.
  • Name your transitions: match cut, whip pan, slow-motion impact freeze, smash cut, cut to.

Why the climax sits at the end

Short-form video is compressed storytelling: introduction → buildup → escalation → climax. Put the payoff — the reveal, the transformation, the hero moment — in the final block. Front-load the climax and you kill the watch-through.

07

Characters: mapping & naming

The moment a clip has more than one character, two techniques keep their identities from bleeding into each other.

1 — Map each character to references

Declare a mapping table before your timed blocks. Split each identity into a face and a body reference and bind them explicitly.

Character mapping
Character mapping: Character A (role) uses @Image1 (face) + @Image2 (body). Character B (role) uses @Image3 (face) + @Image4 (body). Never swap identities.

2 — Name them, and reuse the names

Use stable, numbered proper names and repeat them in every single beat. Generic nouns let the model re-roll a face between shots — that's exactly how identities drift.

✓ Correct

"Ken 1 attacks. Ken 2 blocks."
"Ken 1 and Ken 2 face each other. They draw swords."

✕ Incorrect

"The man attacks."
"Another character responds."

A named, numbered label referenced consistently anchors the subject across the whole clip. It's the cheapest, most powerful consistency tool you have.

08

Referencing methods & options

Reference-to-Video is where Seedance becomes a precision tool. You can feed it stills, videos, or both, and tell it exactly what to take from each.

What you can feed it

InputLimit
Imagesup to 9
Videosup to 3 — each 2–15s, total ≤ 15s
Audiodescribed in the prompt (not uploaded)
Generated length4–15s, your choice

You must supply at least one reference of any kind. Two controls make references precise:

  • Tag your subjects with [@name] — e.g. [@lady rabbit] — so the prompt's actions bind to the right element.
  • Assign each image a role in plain words: "the first image is the character reference, the second is the scene reference." This is the foundation of every multi-reference shot.

The five referencing capabilities

CapabilityWhat it doesThe core move
Image → Video1–9 stills become a moving clip, keeping their look & styleDescribe the motion; [@tag] subjects; assign each image's role
Video → Video1–3 clips drive a new one, keeping subject / action / rhythm / compositionState which attributes to keep; add a still as an appearance anchor if needed
Video EditingChange the picture or audio of an input clip by instructionSay what to change and to what — swap subject, background, lighting, translate text, remove objects
Video MigrationTransfer a quality — motion, style, edit rhythm, camera work — onto new content"the [camera work / style / editing] of the reference video" + your target
Video ExtensionExtend a clip forward or backward (up to 30s total)Describe the new direction, connected to the first/last frame, with key action beats

First & last frames

Hand Seedance an opening still and a closing still, and it interpolates the journey between them. The secret is in the prompt: describe the motion logic of the in-between. "From the still standing pose in the first image, gather energy and transition into the airborne leap of the second" — the clearer that bridge, the smoother the result.

Director's note

A practical pattern for consistency: a single approved hero still as the appearance anchor, plus role-assigned images for face, wardrobe, and any product — each one named in the prompt for exactly what to lock from it. Motion you want from a reference clip belongs in your words (camera direction + per-beat timing), described rather than only shown.

11

The pre-flight checklist

Run this before you generate. Twenty seconds here saves a re-roll later.

  1. Which mode — T2V, I2V, or R2V? (R2V if you have references.)
  2. Front-load the most important block; keep the Scene → Subject → Action → Shot → Light → Camera → Style → Audio order.
  3. Name shot type and camera movement separately — never "cinematic" alone.
  4. Scene carries scale + weather; Subject carries condition + emotional state.
  5. Multiple actions? Use first… then… finally.
  6. Multi-shot? Use bracketed timed blocks, ≤ 5 shots, climax in the final block.
  7. More than one character? Map @face + @body and use numbered proper names every beat.
  8. State the physics — gravity, impact, environmental interaction.
  9. Using references? Assign each a role and [@tag] the subjects.
  10. Audio: dialogue in quotes, SFX matched to the action.
  11. No contradictions; one language throughout.
  12. Read it back as a shot list — would a working director understand it?
The takeaway

You're never describing a picture. You're handing a crew a shot list. Write it the way a director would speak it — and Seedance 2.0 will shoot it.
